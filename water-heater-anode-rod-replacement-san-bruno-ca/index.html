Water Heater Anode Rod Replacement in San Bruno, CA

The anode rod is the part that sacrifices itself so your tank does not rust. Here is how to know when it is spent and what replacement involves.

By the San Bruno Pro Plumbing team

Inside most tank water heaters hangs a rod that few homeowners ever see. It is called the anode rod, and it is the reason many tanks last as long as they do. When the rod wears out, the steel tank is next. Replacing it is one of the cheapest ways to extend a heater's life, when it is done in time.

This guide explains what the rod does, how to know when it is spent and what replacement involves.

How an anode rod protects the tank

A water heater tank is steel with a glass lining. Tiny flaws in the lining expose steel to water, and corrosion begins. The anode rod is made of a metal that corrodes more easily than steel, so it gives up electrons first and sacrifices itself.

As long as the rod has material left, the tank is protected. When the rod is eaten down to its core wire, nothing stands between the water and the steel.

Types of anode rods

Rod typeStrengthsTrade-offs
MagnesiumStrong protection, common in standard tanksWears faster in some waters and can add odor in certain water chemistries
AluminumLasts longer in some conditionsNot suited for every household and can add aluminum byproducts
Aluminum and zincHelps with sulfur odorDifferent wear profile
Powered anodeDoes not wear out the same wayNeeds electricity and costs more

Signs the rod is spent

  • Rotten egg or sulfur smell from hot water.
  • Rusty or discolored hot water.
  • Popping or rumbling noises from sediment.
  • A heater more than a few years old that has never been inspected.
  • A rod, when pulled, that is thin, pitted or mostly bare wire.

Some of these symptoms have other causes, so the rod should be checked, not guessed at.

How we replace an anode rod

  1. Shut down the heaterTurn off power or gas and close the cold water valve.
  2. Relieve pressure and drain a few gallonsOpen a hot tap and drain enough water to drop the level below the rod port.
  3. Remove the old rodLoosen the hex head with a breaker bar. Stuck rods need steady force and sometimes a helper.
  4. Install the new rodWrap the threads with sealant tape, fit the new rod, tighten it and refill the tank.

Replace the rod, or the heater

If the tank is under about eight years old and does not leak, a new rod is a good investment. If it is older and the rod is long gone, the tank may already be rusting inside. Replacing the rod then adds little.

We tell you which situation you are in. For heaters at the end of their life, see water heater replacement. For help with other failures, see water heater repair.

Flushing and rods go together

While the tank is drained for a rod swap, flushing the sediment out of the bottom is easy. Scale and sludge on the tank floor insulate the water from the burner and stress the steel. Cleaning them out improves efficiency and reduces noise.

If your water is hard, the flush is as valuable as the rod. A softener can reduce the scale at its source.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing a stuck rod and damaging the tank threads.
  • Using the wrong rod for your water, which can cause odor.
  • Skipping the pressure relief step and getting sprayed.
  • Leaving out thread sealant.
  • Replacing the rod in a tank that is already leaking.

When to call us

Call if the rod will not budge, if you have no overhead clearance or if your tank has odor and rust. We also check the relief valve, the straps and the drain pipe during the same visit, so the heater is in good shape afterward.

Choosing a rod type

Anode rods are made of magnesium, aluminum or an aluminum and zinc blend. Magnesium protects best and is the usual factory choice. Aluminum lasts longer in some water and does not cause the same smell issues. Powered rods use a small electric current and need no replacement, but they cost more.

If your hot water smells like rotten eggs, bacteria in the tank may be feeding on the magnesium. Swapping to an aluminum or powered rod can help, and so can a tank flush. A plumber can check which applies.

Signs and timing

  • The rod is thin, pitted or reduced to a bare wire.
  • The tank is more than three or four years old and the rod has never been checked.
  • Hot water shows rust or has a metallic taste.
  • The tank has been flushed and the sediment contained bits of rod coating.
  • You have hard water, which wears rods faster.

Many tanks are installed in tight closets, so there is not enough room above to pull a rigid rod. A flexible, segmented rod bends so it can be fed into the tank. We carry both kinds.

Replacing a rod does not repair a tank that has already started to corrode through. It slows future damage. If the tank is old and rusted at the base, replacement is the better use of money.

San Bruno water chemistry

The City of San Bruno's 2023 Water Quality Report lists local groundwater at a pH of 7.27, with a range of 6.86 to 7.55, and an average hardness of 215.6 ppm as calcium carbonate. The report lists San Francisco Regional Water System water at an average pH of 9.2, with a range of 8.4 to 9.8, and an average hardness of 46 ppm.

Those two sources are very different, and the mix at a given tap depends on location. The report says groundwater made up 29 percent of the 2023 supply. The report does not say how long a rod lasts in either water, so the only way to know your rod is to look at it. The same mix of source waters affects homes in Millbrae, though Millbrae reports using only the regional system.

Source type: City of San Bruno 2023 Water Quality Report and BAWSCA member profile. Facts are stated according to each agency's own published page.
