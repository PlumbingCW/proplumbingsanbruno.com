Water Heater Expansion Tank Installation in San Bruno, CA

A dripping relief valve or a bang after the heater fires can mean thermal expansion. Here is what an expansion tank does and why many homes need one.

By the San Bruno Pro Plumbing team

Water expands when it heats, and a water heater heats a lot of it. In an open system, the extra volume flows back into the city main. In a closed system, it has nowhere to go and pressure rises. A thermal expansion tank gives that water somewhere to go.

This guide explains how the tank works, why many San Bruno homes need one and what installation involves.

Why a closed system needs an expansion tank

Many homes now have a pressure regulator or a check valve at the service entrance. That makes the system closed, and thermal expansion creates spikes. Over time, the spikes stress the water heater, the relief valve, fixtures and pipe joints.

A dripping temperature and pressure relief valve is a classic sign. The valve opens when pressure rises, and it drips away the extra water. The repair is not a new valve, but an expansion tank.

How the tank works

The tank has two chambers separated by a rubber diaphragm. One holds water from the system. The other holds air at a set pressure. As water expands, it pushes the diaphragm and compresses the air.

When the water cools or is used, the air pushes the water back out. The pressure in the pipes stays within a safe range.

Signs you may need one

  • The relief valve drips or discharges when the heater runs.
  • Faucets bang or whine after a heating cycle.
  • Pressure on a gauge climbs when the heater fires.
  • Supply connectors or fixtures fail early.
  • The heater was replaced and the old one had a tank.

Installation steps

  1. Check pressureWe attach a gauge to a hose bib to read static pressure.
  2. Set the air chargeThe tank is pre-charged, and we adjust it to match house pressure.
  3. Install on the cold lineWe tee into the cold supply within a few feet of the heater and support the tank.
  4. TestWe run a heating cycle and watch the gauge and the relief valve.

Replace or add: heaters and tanks

A new water heater installation is the best time to add a tank, since the plumbing is already open. If your old heater had one, it may be failing too. A waterlogged tank no longer works, and tapping on it will give a dull sound at the top.

See water heater installation for the full planning list, and water heater replacement for emergency swaps.

Pressure regulators and the expansion tank

High static pressure shortens the life of every fixture and pipe. A regulator brings it down to a safe level, often around 60 to 80 psi. But it closes the system, which makes the expansion tank important.

If your pressure is high and you have no regulator, we can add one. We check both parts together.

Maintenance

Check the tank's air charge every year or two. Tap the tank. The top should sound hollow and the bottom should sound solid. If both sound solid, the diaphragm has likely failed and the tank needs replacement.

A failing tank shows up as relief valve drips and pressure spikes, and it is a cheap fix compared with the damage it prevents.

How the tank is sized and set

An expansion tank holds a bladder or diaphragm with an air cushion on one side and water on the other. Its size depends on the capacity of the water heater and the water pressure in the house. A larger heater or higher pressure calls for a larger tank.

The air side must be set to match the home's static water pressure before the tank is installed. That means measuring pressure with a gauge and adjusting the air with a tire gauge and a pump. Many tanks leave the factory at a standard setting that does not match every house.

  1. Measure pressureAttach a gauge to a hose bib and read the static pressure with no water running.
  2. Set the airWith the tank off the system, adjust the air valve until it matches that pressure.
  3. Mount it securelyInstall it on the cold supply near the heater with a support strap or bracket if the tank is heavy.
  4. Check for leaks and airOpen the supply slowly, bleed air and confirm there are no drips.

Signs yours has failed

A tank that has failed often feels heavy or full of water when tapped, and the air valve may spit water instead of air when pressed. The heater's relief valve may drip, pressure may spike when the heater runs and faucets may hammer. Any of those suggests the cushion is gone.

Replacing the tank is a quick job. Ignoring it risks a leaking relief valve, strained fittings and a shorter heater life. See water heater installation or water heater repair for related work.

Why a closed system needs one

When water heats, it expands. In an open system that extra volume flows back into the city main. Many homes now have a check valve, a backflow preventer or a pressure regulator at the meter that stops water from flowing back. The system is closed, and the extra volume has nowhere to go.

Without a cushion, pressure rises every time the heater fires. The relief valve may open to release it, which wastes water and can damage the valve over time. Fixtures, supply lines and the tank itself take repeated stress.

San Bruno pressure zones

The Bay Area Water Supply and Conservation Agency profile for San Bruno lists approximately 116 miles of water mains from 2 to 18 inches in diameter, 8 storage tanks with 8.3 million gallons of capacity, 8 booster pump stations and 31 pressure regulating stations. The profile does not describe pressure at individual homes, but it shows that the system manages pressure across a range of elevations.

Homes on lower streets can see higher pressure than homes above them, which is why a gauge at your own hose bib is the real answer. Neighboring Millbrae and Burlingame draw from the same regional system through their own turnouts, and each runs its own network.
